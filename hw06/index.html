Riemann & Lebesgue Integration and Normal CDF

Exercise 1 - Riemann and Lebesgue Integration

Two approaches to numerical integration are compared:

  • Riemann approach: partition the domain \([a, b]\) into \(n\) subintervals of width \(h = (b-a)/n\) and approximate the integral as a sum of rectangles or trapezoids: \[\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx \sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i^*) \cdot h\] Three variants are implemented: midpoint (\(O(h^2)\)), trapezoid (\(O(h^2)\)), and Simpson (\(O(h^4)\)).
  • Lebesgue approach: partition the range \([f_{\min}, f_{\max}]\) into \(m\) horizontal levels and sum level * measure of preimage: \[\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx \sum_{j=1}^m y_j \cdot \mu(\{x : f(x) > y_j\})\] For pathological functions like the Dirichlet function, the Lebesgue integral exists while the Riemann integral does not.

Exercise 2 - Numerical Computation of the Normal CDF

The CDF of the standard normal distribution is: \[\Phi(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-t^2/2}\,dt\]

This integral cannot be expressed in terms of elementary functions. By Liouville's theorem, the antiderivative of \(e^{-x^2}\) is not an elementary function, it cannot be written as a finite combination of polynomials, exponentials, logarithms, and trigonometric functions.

The error function provides an exact analytical representation: \[\text{erf}(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \int_0^x e^{-t^2}\,dt\] \[\Phi(x) = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \text{erf}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)\]

Three numerical approaches are implemented and compared:

  • Simpson's rule: direct numerical integration of the Gaussian PDF from \(-8\) to \(x\). Error \(O(h^4)\).
  • Erf series expansion: Taylor series of the error function: \[\text{erf}(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n x^{2n+1}}{n!(2n+1)}\]
  • Rational approximation (Abramowitz & Stegun 26.2.17): maximum error \(|\varepsilon(x)| < 7.5 \times 10^{-8}\), computationally very efficient.

Discussion

  • Riemann vs Lebesgue accuracy: for smooth functions, both converge to the same value. Simpson's rule converges faster (\(O(h^4)\)) than midpoint or trapezoid (\(O(h^2)\)). The Lebesgue approach is less accurate for the same number of partitions but handles discontinuities more naturally.
  • Pathological functions: the Dirichlet function \(\mathbf{1}_\mathbb{Q}\) is not Riemann-integrable (upper and lower sums never agree) but is Lebesgue-integrable with value 0 (rationals have measure zero). Thomae's function is Riemann-integrable (discontinuous only on a set of measure zero) with value 0.
  • Normal CDF methods: the rational approximation (Abramowitz & Stegun) is the most efficient, constant time, maximum error \(7.5 \times 10^{-8}\). The erf series converges quickly for small \(|x|\) but slowly for large \(|x|\). Simpson's rule is straightforward but requires many evaluations for high accuracy.
  • Why erf is necessary: Liouville's theorem proves that \(e^{-x^2}\) has no elementary antiderivative, so numerical methods or special functions are always required for practical computation of \(\Phi(x)\).

Conclusion

The Riemann and Lebesgue approaches to integration are mathematically equivalent for well-behaved functions, but differ fundamentally for pathological cases. Lebesgue integration, by partitioning the range rather than the domain, can assign a meaningful value to functions that are not Riemann-integrable. For the normal CDF, all three numerical methods agree to high precision, confirming the correctness of the implementations. The rational approximation is recommended for practical use due to its efficiency and guaranteed error bound.
