Statistical Independence & Contingency Tables

Theoretical Background

Two random variables \(X\) and \(F\) are statistically independent if and only if: \[P(X = x, F = f) = P(X = x) \cdot P(F = f) \quad \forall x, f\] Equivalently, \(P(F = f \mid X = x) = P(F = f)\) for all \(x, f\).

Given a sample of \(n\) observations, independence is tested using the chi-square test:

  • Observed frequencies \(O_{ij}\): count of observations with \(X = x_i\) and \(F = f_j\).
  • Expected frequencies under independence: \[E_{ij} = \frac{R_i \cdot C_j}{n}\] where \(R_i\) is the \(i\)-th row total and \(C_j\) is the \(j\)-th column total.
  • Chi-square statistic: \[\chi^2 = \sum_{i,j} \frac{(O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}\] Under \(H_0\) (independence), \(\chi^2 \sim \chi^2_{(r-1)(c-1)}\).
  • P-value: \(p = P(\chi^2_{df} > \chi^2_{\text{obs}})\). If \(p < \alpha\), reject \(H_0\) and conclude dependence.

Important: statistical association does not imply causality. A significant chi-square test only indicates that the variables are not independent in the sample, it does not establish a causal relationship.

Discussion

  • Independence vs dependence: under independence, observed and expected frequencies should be close, large deviations indicate association. The chi-square statistic measures the total squared deviation, normalized by expected frequency.
  • Continuous variables: when variables are continuous, they are discretized into bins before building the contingency table. The choice of number of bins affects the result, too few bins lose information, too many bins produce low expected frequencies (violating the chi-square approximation rule: \(E_{ij} \geq 5\)).
  • Association vs causality: a significant chi-square test indicates statistical association, not causality. The classic example: ice cream sales and drowning rates are correlated (both increase in summer) but neither causes the other, a confounding variable (temperature) explains both.
  • Sample size: with very large samples, even tiny and practically irrelevant associations become statistically significant. Effect size measures (such as Cramér's V) complement the p-value by quantifying the strength of association.

Conclusion

The chi-square test of independence is a powerful tool for detecting statistical association between categorical variables. By comparing observed frequencies with those expected under independence, it quantifies how much the data deviates from the independence hypothesis. However, statistical significance must always be interpreted in context: association is not causation, and practical significance may differ from statistical significance.
